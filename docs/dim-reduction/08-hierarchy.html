Hyperbolic Embeddings

A tree has exponentially many nodes at each depth, and a flat plane has only linearly more room at each radius. Hyperbolic space has exponentially more, which lets it hold a tree without crowding.

Every method so far embeds data into Euclidean space: a flat plane, or a flat higher-dimensional space when more coordinates are needed. It is such a common default that it is easy not to notice it as a choice. But Euclidean space has a property that works against some kinds of data: its volume grows slowly. In a plane, the room available at distance r from the origin (the circumference of the circle of radius r) is proportional to r.

Hierarchies, taxonomies, and other branching structures grow exponentially: each level has some constant factor more nodes than the one above it. A binary tree of depth 20 has about a million leaves, and there is no way to place them in a plane so that tree distances are respected. The leaves pile up at the edge however the interior is arranged.

Hyperbolic space, the geometry of constant negative curvature, has exponential volume growth. The circumference of a circle of hyperbolic radius r grows like er, the same kind of growth as a tree. A tree can be embedded in the hyperbolic plane with every node given room and with tree distances preserved up to a distortion that can be made as small as we like.

The Poincaré disk

There are several models of hyperbolic space. The one used most in dimensionality reduction is the Poincaré disk: the interior of the unit circle, with a metric under which distances grow as you approach the boundary. A step near the center covers a small hyperbolic distance, and a step of the same size on screen near the boundary covers a very large one. The boundary itself is infinitely far away.

Escher's Circle Limit woodcuts show this model: the figures are all the same hyperbolic size, and in the Euclidean view they shrink toward the boundary. Figure 1 does the same with a tree.

Figure 1. One balanced tree, laid out with each level a fixed distance beyond the last: Euclidean distance on the left, hyperbolic distance in the Poincaré disk on the right. Under each panel is the gap between two neighboring leaves, in units of one edge's length, measured in that panel's own geometry. On the left the gap shrinks toward zero as the depth grows, so leaves that are many edges apart in the tree sit almost on top of each other. On the right it stays above one edge length at every depth, even though the disk looks more crowded on screen: the crowding is in the drawing of the disk, and the gap is measured in the space it depicts. The hyperbolic levels are spaced a little more than ln(b) apart for branching factor b, which is the least that lets the rim grow as fast as the number of leaves.

The compression near the boundary belongs to the picture and not to the geometry. To someone living in the hyperbolic plane, the shrinking figures near the boundary would all look the same size. The Poincaré disk is a view of hyperbolic geometry from outside, in the way a world map is a view of the sphere: the underlying geometry is consistent, and the flat rendering introduces the distortion.

Embedding data into the Poincaré ball

Nickel and Kiela's 2017 paper "Poincaré Embeddings for Learning Hierarchical Representations" brought this idea into machine learning. They embedded the nouns of WordNet so that distances in the embedding reflect the hierarchy. Euclidean embeddings needed many dimensions to do that well, and Poincaré embeddings did as well or better with far fewer, because the geometry matched the data.

The algorithm is gradient descent on a loss defined with the Poincaré distance, with one change: the parameters live in the unit ball, so the steps are Riemannian gradient steps that account for how the metric varies across the ball. In practice the Euclidean gradient is rescaled by a factor that goes to zero at the boundary, so a point near the edge cannot step over it.

A 2018 follow-up by the same authors used the Lorentz (hyperboloid) model, which is the same geometry in coordinates that are numerically more stable. Later work built variants of t-SNE and UMAP that lay points out in hyperbolic space, which extends the technique from graphs to general dimensionality reduction.

The Poincaré distance between two points u and v in the unit ball is arcosh(1 + 2 · ‖u − v‖² / ((1 − ‖u‖²)(1 − ‖v‖²))). The denominator goes to zero as either point approaches the boundary (norm 1), which is where the distances grow.

Poincaré maps for single-cell data

One application close to ML practice is in single-cell biology. In 2020, Klimovskaia and colleagues published "Poincaré maps for analyzing complex hierarchies in single-cell data," arguing that developmental trajectories are tree-shaped (a progenitor cell divides into daughters, which divide into more specialized cells) and that a hyperbolic layout shows the hierarchy more faithfully than a Euclidean one.

The choice of target geometry acts as a prior on what the data looks like. Euclidean space assumes the data occupies a flat region with the same amount of room in every direction. Hyperbolic space assumes branching, hierarchical structure. When the data matches the assumption the embedding improves, and when it does not, the embedding gets worse.

Shortest paths in the disk

The shortest path between two points in the Poincaré disk is not the straight segment we would draw between them. It is an arc of a circle that meets the boundary at right angles, and it bends toward the center. The effect is strongest for points near the boundary on different sides of the disk: the shortest route between them goes in toward the middle and back out, the way the shortest route between two leaves of a tree goes up toward the root and back down.

Figure 2. The dashed line is the Euclidean segment between the two points and the solid arc is the hyperbolic shortest path. The faint rings are one unit of hyperbolic distance apart. Drag a point toward the boundary (or focus it and use the arrow keys): the arc bows further toward the center and the hyperbolic distance climbs quickly while the Euclidean distance barely changes. With both points near the center the two paths almost coincide.

This is how the disk fits so much structure into a small area on screen. Points in the outer regions are connected to each other through the interior, so they can be placed at any angle around the rim without their mutual distances being forced to match the short gaps we see between them.

What hyperbolic embeddings cost

The parameterization is numerically delicate: near the boundary, small changes in coordinates are large changes in distance, and points can drift out of the valid region. The plots are harder to read than Euclidean ones, because distance on screen is not the distance being preserved. And most ML tooling assumes Euclidean latent spaces, so using hyperbolic coordinates downstream takes extra work.

The approach also pays off only when the data is tree-like. On data without hierarchical structure a Poincaré embedding still runs, but it brings the numerical and interpretive costs of hyperbolic geometry with nothing in return. That is why hyperbolic embeddings show up mostly on WordNet, taxonomies, phylogenetic trees, and developmental trajectories, and rarely on image or audio embeddings.

Other target geometries

Hyperbolic embeddings are the clearest case in this series of the target space being a design choice. Most methods use Euclidean space because it is familiar and the math is convenient, and for some data another geometry fits better.

Related ideas include spherical embeddings, for data with rotational symmetry or a natural norm, and product spaces (Euclidean times hyperbolic times spherical), for data with mixed structure. These are less developed than hyperbolic embeddings and follow the same reasoning.
