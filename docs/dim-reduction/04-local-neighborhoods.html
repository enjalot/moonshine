Preserving Local Neighborhoods

t-SNE, UMAP, and their relatives give up on preserving distances and preserve neighbor lists instead. That trade produces the cluster plots these methods are known for, and it limits what can be read from them.

Preserving distances is a demanding goal. For data on a curved manifold, every method in article 3 had to work to measure distance correctly (along a graph, by a random walk) before it could try to reproduce it. The methods in this article drop the distances and keep only who is a neighbor of whom.

A scatter plot that gets neighbor lists right shows clusters as tight, well-separated groups. One that gets distances right tends to look smoother, with clusters less sharply set apart. For visualization, the neighborhood-preserving methods have become the default.

The cost is that such plots say very little about global geometry. The distance between two clusters in a t-SNE plot is not meaningful, and neither is the size of one cluster relative to another. Both follow from the objective, which has no term for either. Reading a t-SNE or UMAP plot as if its global coordinates mattered is the most common way to be misled by one.

From neighbors to forces

The methods in this family share one mechanism: pull each point toward its near neighbors, and push it away from everything else. They differ in how the pull and the push are defined, what counts as a neighbor, and how the forces fall off with distance. Figure 1 shows the mechanism with the simplest forces we could pick.

Figure 1. Ninety points from five clusters in 30 dimensions, started in a random heap. Each point is tied by a spring to its k nearest neighbors in the original space, and every pair of points repels. With k = 6 the five clusters pull apart within a few seconds. This is a cartoon of the family, with springs of fixed length and repulsion between all pairs; t-SNE and UMAP use different force laws, and Figures 2 and 3 run the real algorithms.

Raise k past the size of a cluster (18 points) and each point's neighbor list starts to include points from other clusters, so springs tie the clusters to each other and they stop separating cleanly. Drop k to 2 and the springs no longer hold a cluster together as one piece. This neighborhood size is usually the most consequential hyperparameter in the family. t-SNE calls it perplexity and UMAP calls it n_neighbors.

LLE and Laplacian eigenmaps: the spectral predecessors

The first neighborhood-preserving methods were spectral. Locally Linear Embedding (Roweis and Saul, 2000) observes that on a smooth manifold each point is approximately a weighted average of its neighbors. LLE solves for those weights, then finds low-dimensional coordinates in which each point is the same weighted average of the same neighbors.

Laplacian eigenmaps (Belkin and Niyogi, 2003) build the neighbor graph, weight its edges by similarity, and use the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian with the smallest nonzero eigenvalues as embedding coordinates. Points that share many neighbors end up close. Nothing in the objective keeps unconnected points apart, apart from a normalization that stops everything from collapsing to one point.

Both have closed-form solutions, and both tend to produce smooth embeddings in which clusters run into each other. The next generation of methods added an explicit push between non-neighbors and switched from eigendecomposition to iterative optimization.

t-SNE: neighbors as probabilities

t-SNE (van der Maaten and Hinton, 2008) describes neighborhoods as probability distributions. For each point i it places a Gaussian at i and reads off the probability that each other point j is its neighbor. The width of each Gaussian is tuned separately so that the perplexity of the distribution, a smooth count of effective neighbors, matches a value the user sets.

In the low-dimensional layout, t-SNE defines the same kind of probabilities with a Student's t-distribution, and it moves the points to minimize the KL divergence between the two sets of probabilities. Two design choices give t-SNE its character. The t-distribution has heavier tails than a Gaussian, which counters the "crowding problem" in which everything tries to collapse toward the middle. And the KL divergence is asymmetric: it penalizes placing true neighbors far apart much more than it penalizes placing non-neighbors close, which is one reason t-SNE's clusters come out tight and well separated even when the data's are not.

The crowding problem comes from how differently volume grows in high and low dimensions. Around a point in 50 dimensions there is room for many neighbors that are all at about the same distance from it and from each other; in 2D there is not. The t-distribution's heavy tails let moderately distant points sit much farther away in the layout, which makes room for the close ones.

UMAP: a weighted neighbor graph, attracted and repelled

UMAP (McInnes, Healy, and Melville, 2018) arrives at a similar place from a different direction. Its derivation uses fuzzy simplicial sets from algebraic topology. The computation is a weighted k-nearest-neighbor graph in which every point has its own distance scale. For a point i, the distance to its nearest neighbor, ρi, is subtracted from all of its neighbor distances, so its nearest neighbor always gets membership 1. What remains is divided by a bandwidth σi, chosen so that the memberships exp(−(d − ρi) / σi) of its k neighbors add up to log2 k. Every point ends up with a neighborhood of the same total weight, whether it sits in a dense region or a sparse one. Because i's membership for j and j's for i usually differ, the two are combined with a fuzzy union, a + b − ab.

In the layout, the similarity of two points at distance d is 1 / (1 + a d2b), a heavy-tailed curve whose constants a and b are fit from the min_dist setting (with both equal to 1 it is t-SNE's kernel). UMAP's stated objective is the cross-entropy between the graph weights and these similarities, and it is optimized by stochastic gradient descent: each graph edge is sampled in proportion to its weight and pulls its two points together, and for every sampled edge a few randomly chosen points are pushed away from one of its ends. That is the pull and push of Figure 1 with specific force laws.

Two common claims about UMAP need qualifying. The first is speed. One epoch costs in proportion to the number of graph edges, about n · k, and the neighbor search is approximate. Early t-SNE computed all n² interactions, but current implementations (Barnes-Hut, FIt-SNE, openTSNE) approximate them and handle millions of points, so which is faster depends on the implementations being compared. The second is global structure. UMAP is often said to preserve more of it than t-SNE. Kobak and Linderman (2021) traced that difference, in the comparisons they reanalyzed, to initialization: UMAP started from a spectral layout of its graph and t-SNE from random positions, and when both were given an informative start they did about equally well. Neither objective has a term for the distance between far-apart points.

The sampled push is also not the exact gradient of the cross-entropy. Damrich and Hamprecht (2021) showed that drawing a fixed number of negative samples per edge makes the repulsion much weaker than the stated loss implies, which is part of why UMAP's clusters are so compact.

What n_neighbors changes

The neighborhood size (n_neighbors, perplexity, k) sets how far the graph can see. A point's edges reach only its k nearest neighbors, so any structure larger than that is represented only through chains of overlapping neighborhoods, or not at all.

Figure 2. UMAP on a dataset with structure at two scales: three families (hue), each made of three sub-clusters of 14 points (shade). At n_neighbors = 4 almost no edge leaves a sub-cluster, and the layout is a scatter of small islands whose arrangement says little about which belong together. At 15 the graph reaches across sub-clusters and each family's islands sit together. At 40 each family is drawn as one group and the families are pushed far apart. The three layouts answer different questions about the same data.

A small k makes the method attend to fine-scale structure, and it will separate anything that is even slightly a sub-cluster. A large k averages over that and shows larger-scale organization. Most real data has structure at more than one scale, so it looks different at different k, and no single value is correct. A reasonable practice is to run several values and trust what persists: structure that appears at only one setting deserves suspicion.

PaCMAP, TriMap, LargeVis: variations on the objective

Several methods adjust the same recipe. LargeVis (Tang et al., 2016) predates UMAP and shares much of its design: an approximate neighbor graph laid out by sampled attraction and repulsion, built for very large datasets. TriMap (Amid and Warmuth, 2019) replaces pairs with triplets: for an anchor a, a near point n, and a far point f, the embedding should place n closer to a than f is. PaCMAP (Wang and colleagues, 2021) argues that attracting near pairs and repelling the rest is not enough to arrange structure at several scales, and adds a third kind of pair, "mid-near" pairs, that attract with moderate strength early in the optimization. Its authors report better preservation of global structure than t-SNE or UMAP.

These are close relatives of t-SNE and UMAP that work on known weaknesses, mostly around global structure. In ML practice UMAP remains the usual default with t-SNE close behind, and the alternatives are reached for when the default gives something visibly wrong.

One dataset under four methods

Running several methods on the same data is a good way to separate signal from artifact. Where the methods agree, the structure is probably in the data. Where they disagree, the difference comes from what each method assumes. Figure 3 does this with the swiss roll from article 3, now with its points gathered into five clusters along the ribbon, and scores each result with the measures from article 1.

Figure 3. Five clusters along a swiss roll in 3D, embedded by PCA, Isomap (k = 10), t-SNE (perplexity 20), and UMAP (n_neighbors = 15). Under each panel: trustworthiness and continuity at 10 neighbors, and the rank correlation between original and embedded distances. PCA looks down the axis of the roll and sees an arc, with some false neighbors where clusters overlap in its view, so its trustworthiness is the lowest. Isomap straightens the arc into a band with the clusters in order. t-SNE and UMAP pull each cluster into a compact group and keep neighborhoods as well as Isomap does, and UMAP has the lowest distance score of the four. Where they put the groups relative to each other, and the shape of each group, come from the optimization; only the membership should be read.
The t-SNE and UMAP in this article are the real algorithms, run in your browser on a few hundred points: exact nearest neighbors, the published gradients, and a PCA initialization for both. Library implementations differ in ways that matter at scale and not here. umap-learn finds neighbors approximately and starts from a spectral layout, and fast t-SNE implementations approximate the repulsion.

What a neighbor plot lets you conclude

When looking at a t-SNE or UMAP plot, it helps to ask what you are allowed to conclude. You can usually conclude that points in the same visual cluster are neighbors in the original space, and often that points in different visual clusters are not. You cannot conclude anything from the shapes, sizes, or relative positions of clusters, from the distance between two specific points, or from a gap in the plot, which may or may not be a gap in the data.

Read that way, these methods are a reasonable default for language model embeddings, image features, and single-cell data. The common misuse is to read the output as a map, with distances and areas that mean something.

Article 6 covers methods that preserve density, which the per-point distance scales of t-SNE and UMAP remove by design.
