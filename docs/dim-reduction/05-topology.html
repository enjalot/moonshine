Preserving Topology

When the shape of the data matters more than where each point lands, topology offers a different kind of summary. Mapper, Reeb graphs, and persistent homology describe data by what is connected to what.

The methods so far place every data point at a location in a plane. Topological methods drop the locations and keep track of the shape: how many connected pieces the dataset has, and whether it has loops, branches, or holes.

Topology is a coarser description than geometry, on purpose. A circle, an ellipse, and an irregular closed curve are the same loop, so a topological description does not change under noise or deformation. For ML data on a curved manifold with complicated local structure, that can be the part worth keeping: the loop or the branch is still there after a retrain, and the coordinates are not.

This article covers Mapper (the most widely used topological method for reducing data), Reeb graphs (the continuous object Mapper approximates), persistent homology (a way to measure which topological features are robust), and topological autoencoders (which carry a topological constraint into a neural network). None of them replaces t-SNE or UMAP as a default view, and they are usually run alongside one.

Mapper: filter, cover, cluster

Mapper, introduced by Singh, Mémoli, and Carlsson in 2007, is the topological method you are most likely to meet in practice. It has three steps.

First, pick a filter function (also called a lens): any function from the data to a low-dimensional space, often just the real line. Common choices are one coordinate, a density estimate, the distance from a reference point, a PCA component, or a learned embedding.

Second, pick a cover of the filter's range: divide it into intervals that overlap their neighbors (or rectangles, for a 2D filter).

Third, cluster within each interval. For each interval, take the data points whose filter value falls in it and cluster just those points, in the original high-dimensional space. Each cluster becomes a node of the Mapper graph. A point in the overlap of two intervals belongs to a cluster in each, and those two clusters are joined by an edge. The overlap is what connects the graph.

Figure 1. Mapper on a figure-eight. Left: the points colored by filter value, with the cover drawn as overlapping bands. Right: the Mapper graph. With horizontal position as the filter, each band that cuts a loop contains two clusters (the upper and lower arcs), and the graph has two loops joined at the crossing, like the data. Switch to vertical position and each band cuts both loops at once, so a band can hold four clusters and the graph is laid out differently, though it still has two loops. Drop the overlap toward 0.05 and edges start to go missing. The density filter has no direction on the page, so its cover is not drawn.

The two knobs that shape the graph are the number of intervals and their overlap. More intervals give finer detail and more spurious splits. More overlap gives more edges and can merge features that should be distinct. In practice the user sweeps both and looks for structure that is stable across settings.

The filter is the main design decision. The same dataset under different filters shows different things: a height filter on a set of head poses can show the rotation axis, a density filter shows where the data concentrates, and a distance-from-center filter shows radial structure. Mapper's output is a graph and not a scatter plot, which has led some to ask whether it is dimensionality reduction at all. We count it as one: the graph is a low-dimensional description of the data.

Mapper workflows often use a learned lens, for example the activation of one neuron or the output of a classifier. The company Ayasdi built a business on running Mapper with well-chosen lenses on industrial datasets. Choosing the lens is where most of the skill in using Mapper goes.

Reeb graphs: the continuous version

Given a smooth manifold and a scalar function on it (think of height on a torus), the Reeb graph tracks the level sets of the function as its value sweeps upward. Connected components of the level set split, merge, appear, and disappear, and the graph records those events. Each branch point of the graph is a critical point of the function.

Mapper is a discrete, noise-tolerant approximation of the Reeb graph for point clouds. The theoretical guarantees come from the Reeb graph, and the practical use comes from Mapper.

Persistent homology: which features are robust?

Persistent homology is not a dimensionality reduction method, but the rest of topological data analysis leans on it. Grow a ball around each data point and slowly increase the radius. At small radii the union of balls is a set of separate dots. As the radius grows the balls overlap, connected components form, and eventually everything fills in. Along the way topological features are born and die.

A connected component is born when its ball appears and dies when it merges into another component. A loop is born when the balls close a ring around empty space and dies when that space is filled. The persistence of a feature is the length of the interval between its birth and its death.

Figure 2. A noisy circle of 60 points. The barcode has one bar per feature, from the radius where it is born to the radius where it dies, and the dashed line marks the slider's radius. The component bars (H0) are short: every point has joined one component by a radius of about 0.06. The loop's bar (H1) is long: the ring closes at that same radius, and the hole survives until about 0.23, when the balls reach the middle of a circle whose radius is 0.3. The component bars are computed exactly; for the loop we draw only the bar of the large ring and leave out the brief small loops between neighboring points.

Persistent homology answers "which features of this dataset are robust?" with a rule that can be audited: features with long bars exist across a wide range of scales and are likely to be structure, and features with short bars are likely to be noise. Libraries like Ripser and giotto-tda compute persistence efficiently for moderately sized datasets. The barcode is not a picture of the data, but it is a summary of its shape that can be used as a diagnostic or as a feature vector for downstream tasks.

Topological autoencoders: a topological loss for neural networks

Topological autoencoders, introduced by Moor and colleagues in 2020, train an autoencoder with an extra loss term that encourages the persistence features of the latent space to match those of the input space. The result is a learned embedding that tries to preserve topology while remaining a parametric function, with what that brings: new points can be embedded without retraining, training runs on a GPU, and the encoder can be combined with other networks.

Topological autoencoders are not a default tool the way UMAP is. Their appeal is the combination: neural methods scale and are flexible but can distort topology in ways that are hard to predict, and topological methods are principled but hard to scale and tune. A topological regularizer on a neural method gets some of both when the training is stable enough.

When to use a topological method

Topological methods are most useful when the shape of the data is meaningful and the positions of individual points are not. Developmental lineages branch and merge; disease progression can trace loops (illness, treatment, remission, sometimes relapse); dynamical systems visit a set of states with particular transitions between them. A t-SNE or UMAP plot of such data can break a loop or a branch into pieces, since nothing in its objective asks for the loop to stay closed. A Mapper graph represents the loop directly.

The cost is that the output is a graph, and graphs are harder to read at a glance than scatter plots. A common workflow uses topology as a companion: UMAP for the overall picture, Mapper alongside it as a structural check. Structure that shows up in both is more likely to be in the data, and a disagreement points at where to look closer.
