Part 10 of 10 · The capstone

Opportunities & Open Problems

Each family in this series answers part of the question "what should a projection preserve?" None answers all of it. This article puts several of them side by side on one dataset and then looks at what none of them does yet.

The nine articles before this one cover more than a century of methods, most of them from the last forty years. Seen together, the methods group into families by what they preserve, and no family covers everything. Often what one method loses is what the next was built to keep.

This article collects observations about where current methods fall short, especially for the use that motivated the series: understanding the latent spaces of large machine learning models. Most of the directions below have been explored somewhere, so we make no claim that they are new. We are trying to say plainly what current practice gets you and where it runs out.

Five methods, one dataset, four measurements

Article 1 introduced measurements for whether a projection kept what it was supposed to. Figure 1 applies them. The data is six Gaussian clusters in 12 dimensions with very different spreads, the situation of article 6 with more clusters. Each method from the earlier articles embeds it, and each embedding gets four scores.

Figure 1. Six clusters in 12 dimensions, tightest to most diffuse, under five methods run in your browser. Trustworthiness (trust) and continuity (cont.) are computed at 10 neighbors; distances (dist.) is the rank correlation of all pairwise distances; density is the correlation of log local radius, as in article 6. The best score in each column is highlighted, and no row holds all four. PCA keeps distances and most of the density, and overlaps the clusters. t-SNE and UMAP separate every cluster and flatten their sizes. densMAP gets the sizes back at a small cost in trustworthiness. Isomap does badly here for a specific reason: the clusters are far enough apart that its neighbor graph is disconnected, so it has no path lengths between them.

The scores make the trade concrete. A method that wins one column does so by spending the two dimensions of the page on that property, and the others pay for it. On a different dataset the numbers would move, and Isomap in particular would recover on connected data like the swiss roll of article 3, but which column each method does best in would mostly stay the same.

The wider design space

Figure 2 extends the comparison to all nine families and to properties we did not measure, such as whether new points can be placed without refitting. These ratings are our judgment from the earlier articles and are not computed.

Figure 2. Our rating of what each family prioritizes (filled), handles without emphasizing (half), or tends to lose (empty). No row is filled all the way across, so a workflow that needs several properties has to combine methods or accept losses.

PCA is strong on global linear variance and readable components and weak on most other things. t-SNE and UMAP are strong on local neighborhoods and weak on distances and density. Mapper preserves topology and produces a graph instead of a scatter plot. Poincaré embeddings are strong on hierarchy. Learned representations are strong on whatever they were trained for.

Different tools suit different jobs, and trouble starts when one method is treated as a default and its built-in choices are forgotten. The most common failure across ML domains is reading the global layout of a UMAP plot as a map. The second is running one method and never cross-checking it against another.

Evaluation is not solved

Trustworthiness, continuity, stress, and Shepard diagrams each measure preservation of one kind of structure. None of them measures whether the embedding helps a person understand the data, which is much harder to evaluate and has had far less attention.

For ML latent spaces the gap is wider. It is not obvious what it would mean for a projection to "explain" a language model's embedding space. The person looking at it is trying to form a mental model: which concepts cluster, which attributes are directions, where the failures are. A projection can score well on trustworthiness and still be of little use for that, for example by placing a meaningful axis at an arbitrary angle.

Better evaluation probably needs task-specific benchmarks: projections scored by how well they support a particular analysis, such as concept discovery, anomaly detection, or counterfactual reasoning. That is harder to build than a metric reported on MNIST.

Composition is underdeveloped

In practice, workflows chain methods: PCA and then UMAP, UMAP for the picture and Mapper as a check on its topology, an autoencoder for compression and t-SNE on its latent space. Each chain patches one method's gap with another. But the chains are assembled by habit. We know of no principled framework for combining methods with different preservation objectives into one view.

One opportunity is a method that jointly optimizes neighborhood, density, and global distance preservation with weights the user can adjust. densMAP is a step in that direction (neighborhood plus density), and PaCMAP is another (neighborhood plus global structure). A version with three or four terms and sliders for their weights would put the trade-offs of Figure 1 in the interface, where today they are fixed inside each algorithm's defaults.

Another is a method that produces several linked views at once, each with a different objective, with points kept consistent across views. The Grand Tour of the 1980s is the closest classical analog, and we have not seen a modern version built on neural encoders.

Domain-specific priors are underused

The learned-representation family showed that matching the likelihood to the data helps. scVI's zero-inflated negative binomial is what makes its embeddings of count data usable. Outside biology, domain-specific priors are rare. We run the same generic UMAP on language model embeddings, code embeddings, and image embeddings, as if they had the same structure.

Language model hidden states have structure we know something about: superposition, polysemanticity, token-level features interacting with concept-level ones, directions reused across layers. A projection method that modeled this, for example an encoder whose loss rewarded separating superposed features, might produce better views for interpretability research than a generic UMAP. As far as we know nobody has built one. Sparse autoencoders address the separation and have not yet been turned into a dimensionality reduction workflow.

Interactive projections are mostly 2D scatter plots

Most methods in this series produce a 2D scatter plot and stop. Interaction, where there is any, is usually hovering for a label or brushing to filter. We have seen what a Mapper graph, a SOM grid, and a Poincaré disk look like, but we do not routinely build interfaces that switch between scatter, graph, grid, and hyperbolic views of the same embedding with the views linked.

Tools like latent-scope, Atlas, and the Embedding Projector move in this direction, and most of them rely on UMAP or t-SNE for the main view. A more ambitious tool would let the user manipulate the projection method itself: choose what to preserve, watch the projection update, compare views side by side, and keep annotations attached to points across projections.

The visual analytics literature on dimensionality reduction, published at IEEE VIS and in TVCG over the last decade, has many of these ideas. Adoption in mainstream ML tools has been slow.

Opportunities specific to large models

The need is sharpest for large models. Hidden states have thousands of dimensions, training runs produce terabytes of activations, and features are superposed and polysemantic in ways that do not show up cleanly in a single projection. Generic methods struggle because the structure we care about is not the structure they preserve. Four directions seem worth pursuing.

Projections that respect the model's own geometry. In a transformer's residual stream, directions matter to downstream layers. A projection that treated directions as the objects being projected, in addition to points, would fit how practitioners already think about these spaces. That is closer to ICA than to UMAP, and it is little explored for modern architectures.

Projections across layers. We rarely look at how one prompt's hidden state changes from layer to layer, partly because few projection methods are designed for sequences of spaces. A method that treated each layer as a time step and produced a consistent 2D trajectory would be useful for interpretability research.

Projections within a subspace. Large latent spaces have subspaces: some directions encode sentiment, others topic, others syntax. A projection that let the user pick a subspace and project within it, holding the rest fixed, would let researchers examine one feature family without the global structure drowning it out.

Projections integrated with probing. Today a researcher runs a probe, reads the result, and separately runs UMAP for a picture. A projection that conditioned on probe directions, or was optimized to keep probe-readable structure visible, would join the two steps.

Combinations the families suggest

Taking each family's main idea and asking what it has not been combined with gives a list of plausible directions.

Hyperbolic layouts for neighbor-graph methods exist at small scale, and a mature hyperbolic UMAP tuned for large-model activations could be useful wherever concept hierarchies matter. Topological regularization, beyond the existing topological autoencoders, could be added to the training of sparse autoencoders. Density-aware methods could move from single-cell biology into tools for comparing model checkpoints, where a change in the density of the activation space says something about training.

Each of these recombines ideas already in this series. What is usually missing is the engineering: building the method, scaling it, building an interface around it, and getting it to the people whose work it could help.

Rules of thumb for practitioners

For someone using dimensionality reduction on ML data, a few habits follow from the series.

Start with PCA. It is cheap and repeatable, and it tells you whether the interesting structure is linear. If PCA already shows what you care about, you may not need anything else.

Run two methods. UMAP with PCA is a reasonable minimum, and UMAP with Mapper or with densMAP is better. Features that both show are more trustworthy, and where they disagree, look closer.

Vary the hyperparameters. Stability across n_neighbors, perplexity, and random seed is how you tell signal from artifact, as Figure 2 of article 4 showed with n_neighbors.

Remember what each method preserves. Cluster sizes in a t-SNE plot, distances between clusters in a UMAP plot, and node positions in a Mapper graph are not meaningful. Each visualization supports fewer conclusions than it appears to.

Ask whether your data has structure that a specific family was designed for. Tree-shaped data suits hyperbolic embeddings, count data suits scVI or something like it, and data with meaningful density differences suits densMAP or PHATE.
