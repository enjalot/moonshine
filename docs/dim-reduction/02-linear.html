The Linear Family

The oldest ideas in dimensionality reduction are often still the right ones. PCA, ICA, NMF, and random projection each describe the data with a small set of directions and differ in what makes a direction good. Kernel PCA runs the same machinery after a nonlinear change of coordinates.

Linear dimensionality reduction is a matrix multiplication. Given data X with n rows and d columns, we pick a skinnier matrix W with d rows and k columns, and the embedding is XW. The methods in this article differ in how they pick W.

That is a narrow design space, and the narrowness is why linear methods are still useful. They are fast, repeatable, and easy to compose, and each output coordinate is a weighted sum of input coordinates that we can read. When a nonlinear method distorts the data in some hard-to-describe way, a linear method is usually the baseline we compare against, and when a nonlinear method needs a starting layout, PCA usually provides it.

PCA: preserve variance

Principal Component Analysis, introduced by Pearson in 1901 and independently by Hotelling in 1933, asks: among all directions through the data, which one has the most spread? That direction is the first principal component. The second is the direction of most spread orthogonal to the first, the third is orthogonal to both, and so on. We order them by spread and keep the top few.

Geometrically, the first few components define the subspace that lies closest to the data in a least-squares sense. If you were asked to summarize the cloud with a line, you would pick the first component; with a plane, the first two.

Raise the stretch and PC1's share of the variance climbs toward 100%; set the correlation to zero and the stretch to 1 and the two directions become arbitrary, because a round cloud has no preferred axis.

As the cloud gets more elongated, the first component captures a larger share of the variance. In the extreme case where the cloud is a line, PC1 captures 100% and a one-dimensional embedding loses nothing. Most real data is somewhere in between: the first few components capture most of the variance, and the rest is treated as noise.

The components are the eigenvectors of the data's covariance matrix, or equivalently the right singular vectors of the centered data, and implementations use the SVD because it is numerically more stable. The variance explained by a component is its eigenvalue, so choosing how many components to keep is choosing a cutoff on a sorted list.

PCA cannot bend. If the structure of the data lies on a curved surface, no flat subspace fits it well: PCA sees a swiss roll as an elongated blob and has no way to unroll it, which is the observation behind almost every nonlinear method in this series. Even so, PCA is a good baseline and a good initialization, and for data that is close to linear (a lot of data is) it is often the answer.

A rule of thumb: if you do not know where to start with new high-dimensional data, look at PCA first. If the first two components tell a reasonable story, you might not need anything else. If they do not, you have learned something about the data's nonlinearity before spending time on harder methods.

ICA: preserve independence

Imagine three microphones in a room with three people talking. Each microphone picks up a mixture of all three voices, with mixing weights that depend on the distances. Can we recover the individual voices from the mixtures alone?

PCA cannot. The principal components of the mixtures are the directions of highest variance, which have no reason to line up with the individual speakers. PCA removes correlation between its outputs, and uncorrelated is a weaker condition than separate.

Independent Component Analysis, developed through the 1990s and widely used in the form of Hyvärinen and Oja's FastICA, makes two assumptions: the original sources are statistically independent, and they are non-Gaussian. By the central limit theorem, a mixture of independent non-Gaussian signals is closer to Gaussian than the sources are. So if we search for the directions in the data whose projections are the least Gaussian, we find the sources.

Figure 2. A sawtooth and a square wave are mixed into two observed signals. PCA's two outputs are uncorrelated but each is still a blend of both sources. ICA's outputs match the sources, up to sign and scale, which ICA cannot determine. The ICA here whitens the mixtures and then tries every rotation angle, keeping the one whose output has the largest excess kurtosis; that is the two-signal version of the search FastICA does iteratively.

Gaussian sources are the case ICA cannot handle. A rotation of Gaussian noise is still Gaussian noise, so nothing distinguishes the original orientation from any other.

ICA is standard in neuroimaging: EEG and fMRI record linear mixtures of underlying brain sources plus artifacts (eye blinks, heartbeat, muscle activity), and running ICA and labeling the components by hand is a routine preprocessing step. In machine learning it is less often the first method tried, but it shows up in feature engineering, denoising, and the analysis of learned representations.

NMF: preserve parts

Suppose your data are pixel intensities, word counts, gene expression levels, or anything else that cannot be negative. PCA will happily write a face as "average face + 0.7 times component 1 (which has negative regions) minus 0.3 times component 2." The math is fine, but it is hard to say what a negative amount of an eye means.

Non-negative Matrix Factorization, in the form Lee and Seung published in 1999, adds one constraint: both the basis patterns and the coefficients must be non-negative. Each sample is a sum, never a difference, of non-negative parts. On face images, Lee and Seung's basis patterns looked like localized features (noses, eyebrows, mouths), and every face was a positive combination of them.

Figure 3. Each of the twelve samples (middle row) adds up some of the four parts (top row) with random positive weights. The bottom row is what NMF recovers from the samples alone, using Lee and Seung's multiplicative updates. At rank 4 it finds the four parts. Lower the rank and parts are merged into one pattern; raise it and a part gets split across several patterns.

NMF is widely used in single-cell genomics, where a cell's expression is modeled as a combination of gene programs; in topic modeling, where documents are combinations of word-distribution topics; and in audio source separation, where spectrograms are sums of non-negative frequency patterns. Where data has a natural "parts" reading, NMF tends to find the parts.

The tradeoffs: the factorization is not unique (many valid decompositions of the same data exist), the optimization is non-convex, and the rank has to be chosen. Unlike PCA, the coefficients are fit under the non-negativity constraint and are not a plain multiplication of the data by a fixed matrix. But when the data is non-negative and the parts mean something, the result is usually one that an analyst can reason about directly.

Kernel PCA: linear methods in a transformed space

PCA assumes the structure in the data is linear. Schölkopf, Smola, and Müller's kernel PCA (1998) handles structure that is not linear but becomes linear after a change of coordinates.

Take a dataset shaped like two concentric rings. No linear projection separates them, because they differ along the radius and the radius is not a direction. But if we map each point (x, y) to (x, y, x² + y²), the two rings sit at different heights and a linear method can separate them. A linear method in the transformed space is a nonlinear method in the original space.

Writing the transformation out becomes expensive in high dimensions, and for some useful transformations the new space is infinite-dimensional. The kernel trick avoids writing it out: PCA only needs inner products between points, and an inner product in the transformed space can be computed from a kernel function k(x, y) applied to the original points. So we compute the matrix of kernel values between all pairs and do PCA on that.

Figure 4. Two rings, and their first two components under PCA and under kernel PCA with a Gaussian kernel. PCA of two-dimensional data can only rotate it, so the rings stay nested. Kernel PCA's first component (horizontal) puts the inner ring at one end and the outer ring at the other, so a single threshold on that axis separates them.

Common kernels are the polynomial kernel (which captures products of coordinates) and the RBF or Gaussian kernel (which captures local similarity). The kernel and its hyperparameters are the knobs. Kernel PCA is also a unifying view: Isomap, Laplacian eigenmaps, and diffusion maps, which we meet in the next two articles, can each be written as kernel PCA with a particular kernel built from the data.

What holds kernel PCA back is scale: the kernel matrix is n-by-n, and eigendecomposing it is cubic in n. Beyond a few thousand points it becomes slow without approximation, and for large data the neighbor-graph methods of article 4 are used instead.

Random projection: skipping the optimization

The last member of the family does not optimize anything. We draw W at random (Gaussian entries work, and so do sparse random signs) and multiply.

The Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma says that a random projection into O(log(n) / ε²) dimensions preserves every pairwise distance among n points to within a factor of 1 ± ε, with high probability and regardless of the original dimension. It works because the length of a vector projected onto many random directions concentrates tightly around its expected value.

Figure 5. A Shepard diagram for a Gaussian random projection of 200 points from 200 dimensions. Each dot is a pair of points: its original distance across, its projected distance up. Dots on the diagonal are preserved exactly. At 16 dimensions the band around the diagonal is wide but still straight; drag toward 100 and it tightens, drag toward 2 and it spreads until short and long pairs overlap.

Random projection is for reducing dimension for speed, not for visualization. As preprocessing before clustering, before nearest-neighbor search, or before a more expensive method, a random projection from 10,000 to 100 dimensions is cheap, loses little by the lemma's guarantee, and has one parameter. Several nearest-neighbor libraries used by the methods later in this series rely on it internally.

What linear methods cannot do

Every method here looks for a linear combination of coordinates that captures the property it cares about: variance (PCA), independence (ICA), additive parts (NMF), similarity under a kernel (kernel PCA), or distance (random projection). They differ in objective and constraints.

Linear methods struggle when the structure is curved. A swiss roll, two interlocking crescents, a branching developmental trajectory: in each of these the local geometry does not match the global geometry, and no single linear projection reconciles them. Kernel PCA gets around this only if we already know a kernel that suits the shape. The next article keeps the idea of preserving distances but changes how distance is measured: along the surface, through a graph, or by a random walk.
