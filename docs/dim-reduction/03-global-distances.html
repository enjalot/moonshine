Preserving Global Distances

If we want the picture to tell us how far apart things are, we need a method that respects distances. But what counts as "distance" is itself a choice, and the four methods in this article make it four different ways.

Linear methods force the data into a flat subspace. That works when the data is already flat, but a ribbon of points that curls through three-dimensional space cannot be laid out on a plane by any rotation. The distance between two points along the ribbon can be much longer than the distance between them through empty space, and it is usually the distance along the ribbon that we care about.

Each method here starts from a matrix of pairwise distances and looks for a low-dimensional layout that reproduces it. Classical multidimensional scaling takes the distances as given. Sammon mapping reweights them so that small distances are preserved more faithfully than large ones. Isomap replaces straight-line distance with distance along the data. Diffusion maps replace it with a distance defined by a random walk.

Classical MDS: given the distances, recover the points

The oldest member of the family, classical multidimensional scaling, takes a distance matrix as input. You hand it the pairwise distances between n items, and it returns coordinates in k dimensions whose pairwise distances match the input as closely as possible.

The classical solution double-centers the squared distance matrix and takes its leading eigenvectors, the same tools as PCA. When the input distances are Euclidean distances between data points, classical MDS gives the same embedding as PCA, reached from the distances instead of the coordinates. MDS is the more general tool because its input does not have to come from coordinates at all: it can be perceptual distances between colors, edit distances between strings, or any other pairwise dissimilarity.

A close relative, metric MDS, drops the eigendecomposition and minimizes stress, the sum of squared differences between input and embedded distances, by gradient descent. Figure 1 uses that version, started from the classical solution, because the next method is a small change to it. The data is four rings in three dimensions, which cannot be flattened into a plane without changing some distances, so the method has to decide which errors to accept.

Figure 1. Four rings at the corners of a tetrahedron, embedded in the plane. In the Shepard diagram each dot is a pair of points, and dots on the diagonal are pairs whose distance survived. Under metric MDS the within-ring pairs (lower left) scatter widely and some rings are squashed nearly flat. Switch to Sammon mapping and the rings open up again, while the between-ring pairs (upper right) are about as far off as before.

Stress treats every pair alike: an error of one unit costs the same whether the pair was one unit apart or ten. With far more long pairs than short ones, the layout is decided by the long pairs, and the short distances inside each ring absorb the error.

Sammon mapping: some distances matter more

Sammon mapping, introduced by John Sammon Jr. in 1969, starts from the observation that not all distance errors are equally bad. If two items are supposed to be 10 units apart and the embedding places them at 11, that is a minor error. If they are supposed to be 0.5 units apart and the embedding places them at 1.5, near neighbors have been pulled apart.

Sammon's stress divides each squared distance error by the original distance, which gives small distances more weight. The optimizer then works harder to get local relationships right and tolerates error in the global layout when it has to. In Figure 1 the mean error on the shortest quarter of pairs roughly halves when you switch to Sammon mapping, and the error on the longest quarter hardly moves, because for these rings the long pairs were already as good as a plane allows.

Sammon mapping sits between the MDS family and the neighbor-based family of article 4. Once local relationships are weighted more than global ones, the next step is to drop the global ones entirely, which is roughly what t-SNE does.

Isomap: measuring distance along the data

Sammon mapping still measures distance in a straight line through the original space, and that is a problem for curved data. The classic example is the swiss roll, a two-dimensional ribbon rolled up in three dimensions. Two points on adjacent layers of the roll are far apart along the ribbon but close through the air between the layers. A method that uses straight-line distance treats them as neighbors and folds the ribbon onto itself.

Isomap, introduced by Tenenbaum, de Silva, and Langford in 2000, redefines distance as the shortest path in a nearest-neighbor graph. Each point is connected to its k closest neighbors, and the distance between two points is the length of the shortest path between them through the graph. On a well-sampled swiss roll this geodesic distance follows the ribbon: the shortest path between two layers has to walk around the roll.

Isomap is classical MDS applied to the matrix of geodesic distances.

Figure 2. Left: a swiss roll and its k-nearest-neighbor graph, colored by position along the ribbon. The dashed line is the straight-line distance between the two marked points and the solid path is the shortest route through the graph. Right: Isomap's embedding, which at k = 6 is the unrolled ribbon. Raise k to 8 and two edges jump between layers (drawn in orange); the shortest path takes the jump, the graph distance drops to the straight-line distance, and the embedding folds over. Click two points, or use the button, to measure a different pair.

The graph that makes Isomap work is also its weak point. If the sampling is too sparse, shortest paths are a poor approximation of distance along the surface. If k is too large, shortcut edges appear between points that are close in 3D but far along the manifold, and a single shortcut shortens every path that can use it. Isomap is at its best on densely sampled, smooth manifolds and gets less reliable as those assumptions weaken.

A workable k for Isomap depends on the intrinsic dimension and the sampling density of the data. Too small and the graph breaks into disconnected pieces; too large and shortcut edges distort the geodesics. In Figure 2 the first shortcuts appear at k = 8. Choosing k is the main piece of craft in using Isomap.

Diffusion maps: distance from a random walk

Isomap measures distance as the length of one shortest path. Diffusion maps ask how many paths connect two points. Two regions connected by many independent paths should count as closer than two regions connected by one thin bridge, even if the bridge is short.

Diffusion maps, developed by Coifman and Lafon in 2006, define distance through a random walk. Release a cloud of walkers at one point and let them wander the neighbor graph, stepping to a neighbor with probability weighted by similarity. After a few steps the cloud has spread out. The diffusion distance between two points compares the two clouds released from them: it is small when the walkers from both points end up in the same places with the same probabilities.

Diffusion distance is robust to noise, since one bad edge has little effect when there are many good ones, and sensitive to bottlenecks, since two well-connected regions joined by a thin bridge are treated as farther apart than their graph distance suggests.

Figure 3. Two clusters joined by a narrow bridge. Nodes are shaded by the probability that a walker who started at the ringed node is there after the given number of steps. After 4 steps from inside the left cluster the walk has covered that cluster and almost none of it has crossed. It takes several dozen steps for a noticeable share to reach the right side, although the bridge is only four edges long. Click any node to start the walk there.

Diffusion maps have been influential in single-cell biology, where they handle branching developmental trajectories well, and in the analysis of dynamical systems, where a random walk is a natural model of how a system moves between states. They are also central to the theory of spectral methods: the embedding coordinates are the leading eigenvectors of the random walk's transition matrix, each scaled by its eigenvalue raised to the number of steps.

Where distance preservation works and where it struggles

All four methods take distances as their primary object and try to reproduce them in two or three dimensions, and each is close to MDS applied to a different distance matrix: Euclidean, reweighted Euclidean, geodesic, diffusion.

When distance is a meaningful quantity (spatial data, feature vectors with calibrated scales, perceptual studies), this family is strong. When the interesting structure is in who is near whom and not in how far apart, there is no reason to spend the two dimensions of the page on reproducing long distances. The next article is about methods that give up on preserving distances and preserve neighbor lists instead, which is the shift behind t-SNE and UMAP.
