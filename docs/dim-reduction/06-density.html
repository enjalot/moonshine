Preserving Density

Most dimensionality reduction methods discard how densely packed each region of the data is. Two methods that keep some of it, densMAP and PHATE, change what the scatter plot can tell you.

Picture a UMAP plot of three clusters: three clouds, roughly the same size, separated by empty space. Now suppose one of those groups was ten times as tightly packed in the original space as the others. The plot would look much the same. All three clouds come out about equally dense on screen, because the layout is driven by who each point's neighbors are and not by how far away they were.

This is a design choice, and for many tasks it is a helpful one, since clusters of similar size are easy to read. But when density is the signal, the choice removes it. A rare cell type that is tightly concentrated because it performs one specific function, a well-practiced region of a policy's state space, a narrow mode of a mixture model: in a standard embedding each of these looks the same as a diffuse, noisy cluster.

Two methods put density back. densMAP is UMAP with an extra term that preserves local density. PHATE, designed for single-cell trajectories, embeds a diffusion-based distance that keeps continuous variation continuous. This article covers what each one does and when it helps.

Why the neighbor-based family flattens density

The flattening happens before the layout starts. As we saw in article 4, UMAP gives every point its own distance scale, chosen so that its neighbors' memberships add up to the same total, and t-SNE tunes a separate Gaussian width for every point to hit the same perplexity. After that rescaling, a point whose ten nearest neighbors are one unit away and a point whose ten nearest neighbors are ten units away have the same neighbor weights. The absolute distances are gone.

During the layout, repulsion between non-neighbors then spreads each cluster out until its points are about evenly spaced. The final plot tells you about cluster membership and not about cluster tightness, which is why article 4 warned against reading cluster sizes in a t-SNE or UMAP plot. This article is about what to do when you want them to mean something.

densMAP: UMAP with a density term

densMAP, introduced by Narayan, Berger, and Cho in 2021, adds one term to UMAP's objective. For each point it computes a local radius: the average squared distance to the point's graph neighbors, weighted by the edge weights. It computes the same quantity in the layout, and the added term rewards correlation between the logarithms of the two across all points. The density term is switched on only for the last part of the optimization, after ordinary UMAP has arranged the clusters.

A dense cluster in the original space then stays compact in the embedding and a sparse one stays spread out. The spacing between clusters is still not meaningful, because repulsion still decides it, but within the plot relative density becomes readable. In biological data, cells that are tightly related cluster tightly, and cells that form a loose continuum show up as a loose continuum instead of being compressed into a blob.

densMAP is a modification of UMAP and not a new method, so it inherits UMAP's speed, interface, and ecosystem. In umap-learn it is a single flag. The densMAP in Figure 1 is a port of that implementation's gradient.

The weight on the density term is a blend: at 0 the output is ordinary UMAP, and as it increases density is preserved more faithfully at some cost to neighborhood preservation. In Figure 1, with the library's default weight of 2, trustworthiness drops slightly while the density correlation rises from near zero to near one.

PHATE: potential distances for continuous structure

PHATE (Potential of Heat-diffusion for Affinity-based Transition Embedding), from Moon and colleagues in 2019, was designed for single-cell developmental biology, where the data is less a set of discrete clusters than a continuum of states that cells pass through as they differentiate. A UMAP plot of such data can carve the continuum into pieces and bend its branches arbitrarily, because its objective only asks that neighbors stay neighbors.

PHATE is built on the potential distance. It starts with diffusion on the neighbor graph, as in the diffusion maps of article 3: a random walker starting at one point has some probability of being at each other point after t steps. PHATE takes the logarithm of those probabilities (the "potential"), measures the distance between two points as the distance between their log-probability profiles, and embeds that distance matrix with MDS.

The log is what separates PHATE from diffusion maps. Without it, the distance between two points is dominated by the few destinations where their walkers are most likely to be. With it, a destination that one walker reaches with probability 0.001 and the other with probability 0.00001 counts as a large difference, so far-away parts of the graph contribute to the distance too. PHATE embeddings therefore reflect both local and global structure, and branching trajectories come out as branches.

Middle: UMAP at n_neighbors = 10 pulls the branches apart and breaks some into fragments, and nothing in the layout shows that they share an origin. Right: a simplified PHATE keeps the three arms joined at the origin and about equal in length. The number under each panel is the rank correlation between layout distance and distance along the branches. Neither layout keeps the widening toward the tips; PHATE's diffusion smooths it away. This PHATE uses a fixed 16 diffusion steps and a plain adaptive kernel, where the library picks the number of steps from the data and uses a faster approximation.

When density matters

Density preservation matters most when the data is a continuum or when relative density is the biological or physical signal. The standard examples are in single-cell biology: developmental trajectories where cells move from stem to specialized states, immune responses where T-cell populations expand and contract, disease progression where patients trace paths through a state space. In each, how many cells are concentrated in a region carries meaning.

In ML, density is less often the main concern, but it comes up. When comparing two embeddings (before and after training, say), we may want to know whether the feature space is becoming more or less concentrated in some regions, and a density-preserving view shows that change where a flattening one hides it. When studying the representations of a model with many classes, the relative concentration of samples per class can be a useful diagnostic.

What density methods give up

Density-preserving methods pay for it with less separation between clusters. A diffuse cluster drawn at its true relative size takes up room and can overlap its neighbors, so dissimilar groups sometimes look merged. We gain information about each cluster's internal structure at some cost to the clarity of the boundaries between clusters.

A sensible workflow is to run both a standard neighborhood method and a density-preserving one and read them together: the first shows where the clusters are, and the second shows how tight each one is.

Every method so far produces a scatter plot in which each point is free to land anywhere. The next article is about a family that fixes the output positions in advance: a self-organizing map assigns every data point to a cell of a grid.
