Part 1 of 10 · What Dimensionality Reduction Preserves

The Geometry of High-Dimensional Data

Before comparing methods, we need to agree on what they are trying to preserve. This article builds intuition for high-dimensional space and for the different kinds of structure a dimensionality reduction method might care about.

An image classifier produces a 2048-dimensional feature vector. A language model produces a 4096-dimensional residual stream activation. A single-cell RNA sequencing assay produces a 20,000-dimensional expression profile. Each row of such data sits in a space we cannot draw, and dimensionality reduction exists to give us a picture of it. The question for this series is what that picture should be a picture of.

Every dimensionality reduction method throws information away. What separates methods is which properties each tries to keep and which it is willing to sacrifice. t-SNE, UMAP, PCA, PHATE, and Mapper each answer a different question about the data, so choosing between them depends on your data and your goal more than on which scatter plot looks best.

The later articles take the families of methods one at a time, grouped by what they preserve. This one sets up the vocabulary they share: how distances behave in high-dimensional space, what kinds of structure a dataset can have, and how we can measure whether a projection kept them.

How distances behave in high dimensions

Most of our visual intuition for points, distances, and neighborhoods comes from two- and three-dimensional space, and some of it stops working in the spaces where ML data lives.

Consider a uniform random cloud of points and measure the distance between every pair. In two dimensions those distances are spread out: some pairs are close, some are far. As the dimension grows the distances bunch up, until every pair of points is roughly the same distance apart. Figure 1 shows the two-dimensional histogram as an outline and the histogram for the slider's dimension filled in.

Figure 1. Pairwise distances between uniformly random points in a unit cube, as a fraction of the cube's diagonal. The outline is two dimensions; the filled bars are the dimension on the slider. Drag it upward and the bars narrow toward a single spike, while the ratio between the farthest and nearest pair (top right) falls toward one.

In three dimensions the nearest point is clearly closer than the farthest. In fifty dimensions, with points drawn from the same uniform distribution, the ratio between them shrinks toward one, so "the closest point" carries much less information than it did. This effect is called the concentration of distances, and it is one piece of the curse of dimensionality.

Real data is rarely uniform. A language model's embeddings do not fill their 4096-dimensional ambient space. They cluster, they trace curves and surfaces, and they leave most of the space empty. The meaningful variation lives on a much lower-dimensional structure embedded in a high-dimensional container, and dimensionality reduction tries to find that structure and draw it at a size we can look at.

The intrinsic dimension of a dataset is the number of parameters needed to describe it, which is often far smaller than the number of coordinates it is stored in. A rotating 3D object recorded as 100-pixel-by-100-pixel frames has 10,000 coordinates but only a few degrees of freedom. Estimating intrinsic dimension is its own research area.

What counts as "structure"?

If the goal is to preserve structure, we should say what structure means, and there is more than one answer. The same dataset has several kinds of structure at once, and different methods put different kinds first.

Take a two-dimensional dataset of two interlocking crescents. Figure 2 draws it five ways. Each view emphasizes one property and ignores the others, and a dimensionality reduction method has to decide which of them to protect when the data gets squeezed into fewer dimensions.

Figure 2. One dataset, five kinds of structure. In the distances panel, the circle around the marked point holds its 40 nearest points by straight-line distance, and 12 of them belong to the other crescent. In the neighbors panel each point is joined to its five nearest neighbors, and no edge crosses from one crescent to the other.

The points view shows the raw coordinates: two crescents, each a different color.

The distances view picks one point at the tip of the blue crescent and draws a line to every other point, darker for shorter distances. Several orange points are closer to it than the far end of its own crescent is. A method that tries to preserve all pairwise distances will have a hard time with this shape, because two points can be close in a straight line and far apart along the crescent.

The neighbors view draws edges only from each point to its five nearest neighbors. The edges trace the two crescents and none of them joins one crescent to the other, which is closer to how we would describe the shape ourselves. Many of the methods we will meet later, t-SNE and UMAP among them, start from a neighbor graph like this one instead of from raw distances.

The density view shades the plane by how many points are nearby. Most methods discard this information: they lay clusters out so that they are visually separated and similar in size, even when one is much denser than another.

The topology view ignores metric detail and records only what connects to what: two connected components, no loops. This is a lossy summary, but it survives stretching and bending, and it is what a method like Mapper is designed to preserve.

No single projection keeps all five views faithful. When a method collapses 4096 dimensions to 2 it has to give some of them up, and the rest of this series is organized by which ones each family keeps.

Measuring what a projection kept

Once we know what a method is trying to preserve, we can ask whether it succeeded. A few standard measurements each check a different property.

Trustworthiness asks: of the points that appear close in the 2D picture, how many were close in the original space? A trustworthy embedding does not invent neighbors. Continuity asks the reverse: of the points that were close in the original space, how many are still close in the picture? A continuous embedding does not tear apart neighborhoods that belonged together. A projection can score high on one and low on the other, so the two are usually reported together.

Stress measures how well all pairwise distances agree between the original space and the embedding. It suits a method that targets global distances (like MDS), and it is unfair to one that deliberately warps distances in favor of neighborhoods (like t-SNE). A Shepard diagram plots original distance against embedded distance for every pair, which shows where the projection distorts and by how much.

None of these numbers measures quality on its own, because quality depends on what you wanted to preserve. We use them from article 3 onward, and in article 4 and the capstone we compute them for several methods on the same data.

The order runs roughly from the simplest idea (preserve variance along orthogonal directions) to the most open-ended (train a neural network whose latent space is the reduction). Each family adds something the previous one could not do: measure distance along a curved surface, preserve neighborhoods in place of distances, preserve topology in place of geometry, preserve density, fit the data to a grid, embed into a curved space, learn the mapping from data.

Each article can be read on its own, but they build on each other. The linear family sets up the problems that the nonlinear families try to solve. The local-neighborhood family produces plots that are easy to misread, and the topology and density families respond to two of those misreadings. The learned-representation family borrows objectives from all of the others.
