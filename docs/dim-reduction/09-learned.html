Learned Representations

When the dimensionality reduction is a neural network, what it preserves is set by the objective it is trained on. That makes the objective a design choice, and it brings the costs of training a model.

Each method in the earlier articles fixes its notion of a good embedding in the algorithm. PCA preserves variance. t-SNE preserves neighborhoods. Mapper preserves connectivity through a filter. The objective is chosen by the method's designer, and the user's knobs are hyperparameters that change how it is applied.

The learned-representation family trains a neural network to produce the embedding, where the earlier methods optimize the embedding coordinates directly. The network is a function from high-dimensional input to low-dimensional output, and whatever it is trained to minimize becomes what the embedding preserves. The objective is now something the user can choose.

Several methods fit this description. Autoencoders preserve what is needed for reconstruction. Variational autoencoders add a prior on the latent space. Parametric UMAP trains an encoder on UMAP's neighbor objective. Domain-specific methods like scVI reconstruct under a likelihood model suited to the data. Each has an encoder that reduces dimension, an objective that shapes what the encoder keeps, and a latent space that reflects that objective.

The autoencoder

The simplest member of the family is the autoencoder: a network trained to take an input, squeeze it through a low-dimensional bottleneck, and reconstruct the input from the bottleneck. If the bottleneck has two or three dimensions, the encoder half is a dimensionality reduction method, and the bottleneck value is the embedding.

The objective is reconstruction error, usually mean squared error or cross-entropy. The network has to arrange the latent space so that the decoder can recover as much of the input as possible from as few numbers as possible. PCA minimizes the same reconstruction error over linear encoders and decoders. An autoencoder can also learn nonlinear ones, and that is the reason to use it.

One way to picture a trained autoencoder is through its decoder. The decoder maps each latent value to a point in the original space, so as the latent value varies, the decoder's output traces a curve (or, with more latent dimensions, a surface) through the data.

Figure 1. An autoencoder with a one-number bottleneck, trained in your browser on 200 points along a curve. The solid curve is everything the decoder can produce, and it bends into the data's shape as training runs. Points are colored by the latent value the encoder gives them, and the color runs in order along the curve. The dashed line is PCA's reconstruction from one component, the best any straight line can do. The readout compares the two reconstruction errors: the autoencoder ends about three times lower, close to the noise in the data. Move the slider to send one latent value through the decoder.

What an autoencoder preserves depends on its architecture and its training data. With enough capacity it can memorize the data. With too little it reconstructs nothing well. In between it keeps whatever is cheapest to reconstruct: for the curve in Figure 1, a small network finds the position along the curve and discards the scatter around it.

Variational autoencoders: a prior on the latent space

A plain autoencoder puts no constraint on the geometry of its latent space. The encoder can scatter inputs however it likes as long as the decoder can invert the mapping, which often leaves holes, stretched regions, and uneven density. Decoding a random latent point may give nonsense, because the decoder was trained only on the latent points the encoder produced.

The variational autoencoder, from Kingma and Welling's 2013 paper, adds probabilistic structure. The encoder outputs a distribution (the parameters of a Gaussian) in place of a single point, and a KL-divergence term in the loss keeps that distribution close to a fixed prior, typically a unit Gaussian. The latent space becomes continuous with predictable density: we can sample from the prior, decode, and get something that resembles the training data.

So a VAE preserves reconstruction and a smooth probabilistic latent geometry. That is useful for generating samples, for interpolating between examples, and for feeding the latent space to downstream probabilistic models. It is less suited to pure visualization, because the pull toward the prior can blur cluster structure that an unregularized method would keep sharp.

Parametric UMAP: a borrowed objective

If a network learns whatever objective we train it on, we can train it on the UMAP objective in place of reconstruction. Parametric UMAP (Sainburg, McInnes, and Gentner, 2021) does that. It builds UMAP's neighbor graph as usual, and where UMAP would move the 2D coordinates of each point directly, it trains an encoder whose outputs are scored by the same attract-and-repel loss over batches of graph edges. No decoder is needed, though one can be added.

This combines UMAP's preservation of local neighborhoods with the properties of a parametric function. A new data point is embedded with one forward pass instead of a new optimization. The encoder can be regularized, combined with a classifier, or fine-tuned. And once trained, the mapping is fixed: the same input always lands in the same place, where rerunning UMAP gives a new layout each time.

The cost is the cost of training neural networks. You need a GPU or patience, an architecture, and tuned learning rates and batch sizes, and two training runs can still differ. For workflows where the same model will embed new data many times, that is often worth paying. IVIS (Szubert et al., 2019) is a related method that trains its encoder with a triplet loss.

A parametric method turns dimensionality reduction into a trained model that can be saved and called. That suits a pipeline that has to place new examples quickly and consistently. The non-parametric defaults, t-SNE and UMAP, are closer to a one-off analysis of a fixed dataset.

scVI: a likelihood made for count data

A learned representation also lets us choose the likelihood model. Consider single-cell RNA sequencing. Each cell gives a vector of counts for roughly 20,000 genes, each count between zero and a few thousand. The data is sparse (most entries are zero), discrete, and overdispersed (the variance is much larger than the mean).

PCA and UMAP on raw counts work poorly, since both treat the data as roughly Gaussian vectors under Euclidean distance. The standard workaround is to normalize, log-transform, and then reduce, which hides the count structure and can introduce artifacts of its own.

scVI, from Lopez and colleagues in 2018, is a VAE whose reconstruction term is a zero-inflated negative binomial likelihood, a distribution suited to sparse, overdispersed counts, in place of a Gaussian. The latent space captures biological variation, the likelihood accounts for the count noise, and batch effects are modeled by conditioning on the batch. The pipeline is one probabilistic model where it used to be a sequence of preprocessing steps.

scVI is the clearest example of what the learned family offers: when the data has a specific structure (counts, sequences, images, graphs), the model can be built to respect it. The general-purpose methods of the earlier articles have no place to put that knowledge beyond the choice of a distance.

One architecture, different objectives

For all their differences, the methods in this article have the same parts: an encoder, a latent space, usually a decoder, and a loss.

Figure 2. Four members of the family. The encoder and the latent bottleneck are the same in each; what changes is the loss, written under each row, and where it is applied. Parametric UMAP's loss acts on the latent directly, so its decoder is optional and drawn dashed.

Because the methods differ mainly in the objective, objectives can be combined. A VAE can carry a topological regularizer (the topological autoencoders of article 5). A parametric UMAP can embed into hyperbolic space. This family is modular in a way the others are not.

What learned methods cost

Training takes longer and needs more tuning: minutes to hours on a GPU, against a few minutes for UMAP on a CPU at moderate scale. Results depend on random seeds, initialization, and architecture, and are more sensitive to hyperparameters than the classical methods. Interpretation is harder too, since explaining why two points ended up close in a network's latent space is more difficult than explaining it for PCA.

For visualization alone, UMAP on the raw data is usually faster and nearly as good. For embedding new data, for domain-specific likelihoods, or for composing with other neural components, the learned family is worth its cost. The choice depends on the workflow more than on which method scores better.

Projecting a model's activations

If you study the internal representations of a large neural model (an LLM, a vision transformer, a protein model), you are already looking at a learned representation. The activations are the output of an encoder trained on some objective, and that objective determines what they preserve.

So running UMAP on a language model's embeddings composes two reductions: the model's, trained to predict the next token, and UMAP's, which keeps neighborhoods.
