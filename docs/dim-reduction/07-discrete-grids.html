Self-Organizing Maps

Every other method in this series produces a scatter plot of freely placed points. A self-organizing map requires the output to be a grid, which makes it the method to use when the grid itself is what you need.

The methods so far share a shape: the input is a high-dimensional dataset, and the output is a cloud of points in two or three dimensions, each point free to land wherever the optimization puts it. In a self-organizing map (SOM) the output is a regular grid, rectangular or hexagonal, and the algorithm decides which grid cell each data point belongs to. Points do not get coordinates of their own; they are assigned to cells.

That is a real restriction, and it buys something. A grid is easy to read, easy to tile, and easy to use as a coordinate system for something else. When we want a map-like surface onto which data can be indexed, sorted, compared, or displayed, a SOM provides one. When we want a picture of the data's shape, UMAP or t-SNE is usually the better choice.

Training a grid of prototypes

SOMs were introduced by Teuvo Kohonen in 1982 and are trained by a procedure called competitive learning.

The model is a grid of prototype vectors. A 20-by-20 grid has 400 prototypes, each with the same dimension as the data. They can be initialized randomly or along the top two principal components. Each prototype has a fixed position on the grid, and only its high-dimensional values change during training.

Each training step picks a random data point and finds the prototype closest to it in the high-dimensional space, called the best matching unit or BMU. The BMU moves a little toward the data point. So do the BMU's neighbors on the grid, by an amount that falls off with grid distance. Over the course of training the learning rate decays and the neighborhood radius shrinks, so updates become smaller and more local.

The neighborhood update is what organizes the map. Because prototypes that are near each other on the grid are moved together, they end up similar in the data space, and cells that are far apart on the grid end up different. The layout of the grid comes to reflect the similarity structure of the data.

In two dimensions we can watch the grid bend and stretch to fit the data. In high dimensions the same thing happens where we cannot see it, and what we can look at is the right-hand panel: the grid, with something about each cell's prototype drawn in it.

The U-matrix

A trained SOM is a grid of prototypes that live in the original high-dimensional space. The usual way to see cluster structure in it is the U-matrix: shade each grid cell by the average distance between its prototype and the prototypes of its grid neighbors.

Where neighboring prototypes are similar (low values), the grid is covering a cluster. Where they are dissimilar (high values), the grid is stretched across a gap between clusters. A U-matrix reads like a relief map: heavily shaded "walls" separate pale "valleys," and each valley is a coherent group in the data.

Figure 2. A SOM trained on three Gaussian clusters in 20 dimensions. Left: the U-matrix, each cell shaded by the average distance from its prototype to its grid neighbors' prototypes. Right: the data points placed in their best-matching cells and colored by cluster. The heavily shaded bands on the left line up with the empty cells between clusters on the right: the SOM was never told the cluster labels, and the walls are where its grid had to stretch from one cluster to the next.

When a grid is what you want

A SOM is worth using when the output has to be a fixed surface that can be tiled or printed. Laying documents out on a grid for navigation, or filling a rectangle of small multiples, are cases where a SOM gives a coherent arrangement and a scatter plot would have to be binned after the fact.

It is also a quantization of the data into a fixed number of representative vectors that vary smoothly across a low-dimensional grid. That is useful for coarse-graining a large dataset, where each prototype stands for a neighborhood of data points, or for downstream methods that need a dictionary of representatives.

And it gives a different exploratory view. Because clusters are spread across the grid, the map shows which clusters border which as well as showing that there are clusters.

The best-known application, WEBSOM in the late 1990s, organized large collections of Usenet articles on a SOM grid that users could browse spatially. The same idea appears in current tools that tile embeddings of documents or images into a browsable grid.

What a SOM does not give you

A SOM gives each data point a cell, not a position. The layout within a cluster is not shown, and pairwise distances and fine topology are lost below the resolution of the grid. New points can be placed without retraining by finding their BMU, but the placement is quantized to a cell.

Training also needs care. Random initialization can be slow to untangle, and initializing along the principal components is more reliable. The result is sensitive to the schedules for the learning rate and the neighborhood radius. Extensions such as Growing SOM and Growing Neural Gas add units where the data needs them, at the cost of more machinery.

The growing variants trade the fixed grid for flexibility, and the fixed grid is what makes a SOM distinctive. If you need a layout that adapts to the data, UMAP or another method from the earlier articles is the more direct choice.

SOMs are not a default for exploratory dimensionality reduction. Handed a new high-dimensional dataset and asked for a visualization, we would reach for PCA or UMAP first. A SOM in practice usually means that someone needed a grid.
