Latent Verse / Part 5

The Atlas

A parametric UMAP gives every text a permanent address. Paired with an SAE, it lets you name the regions of the map.

By Ian Johnson

The problem with local maps

In Article 2, we saw that UMAP gives you a map of your data. But that map is disposable. Add new data, re-run UMAP, and the whole layout reshuffles. The neighborhoods stay roughly intact, but every point moves. Two UMAP plots of overlapping datasets can't be compared directly, because they don't share a coordinate system.

This is fine when you just want to explore one dataset. But it creates problems when you want to do any of these things:

  • Compare the structure of two different datasets
  • Add new texts to an existing map without rebuilding it
  • Ask where a single piece of text lives in relation to all text
  • Share a map with someone else and have it mean the same thing to both of you

What we want is a permanent coordinate system. A map that doesn't change when the data changes. A map where every text already has an address.

Training a permanent projector

Instead of running UMAP from scratch on each dataset, train a neural network on the UMAP objective over millions of diverse embeddings. The basemap in this article was trained on 50 million all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings of web text, the same model and corpora as the SAE in Part 4. The network learns a function: embedding goes in, two coordinates come out.

Once trained, this network can project any new embedding instantly. It doesn't need to see the whole dataset or recompute anything. Every text gets a consistent position on a permanent map, shaped by the corpus the network trained on.

This is called parametric UMAP. "Parametric" means the projection is defined by the learned parameters of the network, rather than by the specific dataset you're looking at. Once you have a trained network, you have a basemap: a global map of meaning that any new text can be placed onto.

The local UMAP uses all its space to spread these 400 jokes out, and it keeps more of their neighborhoods. Of each joke's ten nearest neighbors in the embedding, 32% are also among its ten nearest on the local map, against 18% on the basemap (2.5% would be chance). The colours show the same thing: local clusters break up on the basemap. The basemap gives up some local detail in exchange for coordinates that never change.

The jokes also don't sit in one corner of the basemap reserved for jokes. They spread across most of it, next to the web text they share a subject with. The basemap arranges text by what it is about, and dad jokes are about everything.

What lives where

Colour the basemap by one SAE feature and you can see where the jokes that activate it sit. Part 4 split features into specific ones, which fire on a small share of the jokes, and broad ones, which fire on a large share. Pick one of each:

Figure 2. The basemap coloured by one SAE feature. Coloured points are jokes that activate it, out of all 52,573, darker for stronger activation (up to the 1,500 strongest). Grey points are 4,000 random jokes and, fainter, 12,000 of the basemap's training texts. Spread is the median distance between the coloured jokes divided by the median distance between random jokes.

The specific features light up a few dense spots, with stragglers between them. Most cheese jokes gather in two spots, and most astronaut jokes in one. Cheese has a spread of 0.05: its jokes are, at the median, 5% as far apart as two random jokes. Superheroes (0.24) and Christmas (0.32) have more stragglers, likely because many jokes that mention them are mainly about something else.

The broad features light up the whole map. "Definitions and meanings" fires on 15,824 jokes with a spread of 1.0, as scattered as the jokes themselves. These features describe the form of a joke, such as a "what do you call…" question or a story, and the basemap does not arrange text by form. "Fatherhood roles", which fires on mentions of dads and moms, falls in between.

So the basemap gives every text a position, and the SAE's specific features let you name regions of it by what concentrates there. The broad features are a reminder of what the map's layout leaves out.

The full microscope

Now we have all the pieces of the instrument:

  • The lens (embedding model) turns text into a high-dimensional point.
  • The projector (basemap) gives that point a position on a permanent map.
  • The filter (SAE) decomposes the point into interpretable features.

An atlas of meaning

An atlas pairs maps with a vocabulary: the names of places, the features of terrain, the categories that help you navigate. The basemap provides the geography. The SAE taxonomy provides the vocabulary. Together they form a system for understanding what an embedding model has learned.

Different embedding models produce different atlases, just as different lenses show different things. Within any one atlas, though, a dad joke, a news article, and a line of code all get a position and a set of features described in the same terms.

Where to go from here

This series has walked through the components of a latent space workbench: embedding, projection, clustering, feature decomposition, and global mapping. Each piece is interchangeable. You can swap in a different embedding model, try a different clustering algorithm, train a basemap on a different corpus, or use a different SAE architecture.

We used dad jokes because they're fun and small. The same tools work on scientific literature, legal documents, customer feedback, or code, and Latent Scope runs this pipeline on your own data.
