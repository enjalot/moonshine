Latent Verse / Part 2: the projector

Projecting embeddings to 2D with UMAP

UMAP flattens high-dimensional embeddings into two dimensions so you can see a whole collection at once.

By Ian Johnson

Why project to two dimensions?

In the previous article, we looked at embeddings one at a time or in pairs. We could search for similar jokes, but we couldn't see the space they live in. Each joke is a point in 384-dimensional space. We need a way to flatten that space onto a surface our eyes can actually read.

This is what dimensionality reduction does. It takes high-dimensional data and projects it down to two dimensions, trying to preserve the relationships between points. Points that were close in 384 dimensions should stay close in 2D. Points that were far apart should stay apart.

The algorithm we use is called UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection). Here are 1,000 dad jokes, projected from 384 dimensions down to two. One joke is ringed, and the ten jokes most similar to it by cosine similarity, measured in the full 384 dimensions, are orange:

Try a few jokes. With only 1,000 of them the map has no obvious islands, but a joke's nearest neighbors mostly land in its own part of the map, and they tend to be about the same thing. The model's sense of meaning creates this structure, and UMAP makes it visible.

What UMAP preserves and what it doesn't

UMAP tries to preserve local neighborhoods. If two jokes are close in the original 384-dimensional space, they'll tend to land in the same part of the 2D map. It can't keep all of them: there isn't room in two dimensions for every joke to sit next to all of its neighbors. On this map, on average 2 of a joke's 10 nearest neighbors in 384 dimensions are also among the 10 dots closest to it. Picking 10 dots at random would get 0.1.

UMAP also distorts global distances. The distance between two far-apart groups on the map doesn't tell you much about how different those topics actually are. UMAP can place unrelated groups near each other or related groups far apart, depending on how the algorithm resolves the compression from 384 dimensions to 2.

So read a UMAP plot by its neighborhoods. If two points are close, they are likely similar. If they are far apart, you can't conclude much.

Changing n_neighbors and min_dist

UMAP has two key parameters that change the character of the projection:

n_neighbors controls how many nearby points UMAP considers when deciding where to place each point. Low values (5-10) emphasize very local structure, creating tight, separated clusters. High values (30-50) capture broader patterns, producing more connected, flowing shapes.

min_dist controls how tightly points can pack together. Low values (0.01) allow dense clumps. High values (0.5) spread points out more evenly.

Figure 2 has five projections of the same 1,000 jokes, computed ahead of time: three values of n_neighbors, and three values of min_dist at the largest n_neighbors. The joke selected in Figure 1 and its neighbors stay marked, so you can follow them from one layout to the next.

Loading the projections…

Figure 2. The same jokes under five UMAP settings. Every dot moves when the setting changes, and the orange neighbors mostly travel with the ringed joke. With n_neighbors at 5 the dots gather into many small clumps; with min_dist at 0.5 they spread into an even disc.

There is no single "correct" projection. Each parameter setting reveals different aspects of the data's structure. Tight clusters are useful for finding distinct groups. Spread-out projections show gradients and transitions between topics. They also keep fewer neighbors: at min_dist 0.5 the average falls to about 1 in 10, against 2 in 10 for the other four settings.

Why the map changes when the data changes

UMAP is computed fresh for each dataset. If you add new jokes and re-run UMAP, the whole map reshuffles. The neighborhoods stay mostly intact, but the overall layout changes, as it did between the settings in Figure 2. Your map of 1,000 jokes looks nothing like your map of 53,000, even though the same jokes appear in both.

This makes UMAP a local tool. It gives you a map of this dataset, not a map of language in general. In Part 5, we'll see how to build a permanent map that gives every text a consistent address. But first, let's find structure in the map we have.
