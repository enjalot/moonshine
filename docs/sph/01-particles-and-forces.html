Particles, Neighborhoods, and Forces

How a fluid can emerge from particles that only know about their neighbors.

Part 1 of Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics

Particles Instead of Grid Cells

One way to simulate a fluid is to divide space into a grid and track how much fluid is in each cell. The simulation below moves dye and velocity from cell to cell through a fixed grid, and you can drag across it to inject more of both.

Grid-based fluid. A dye source circles the middle of the grid and the swirl it leaves is carried from cell to cell. Drag across the grid to add dye and push the flow. Every cell is updated on every step, including the ones with no dye in them. The grid approach works well for enclosed flows but struggles with splashing, free surfaces, and mixing.

Grid simulations are powerful, but every cell exists whether there's fluid in it or not. Another way is to forget the grid and model the fluid as a collection of particles, each carrying mass, position, and velocity. Particles only exist where the fluid is.

If you've played with a flocking simulation before, the particle approach feels similar. In flocking, each bird looks at nearby birds and follows three rules: separate, align, cohere.

Flocking (boids). One boid is ringed. The dashed circle is its neighbor radius and the three lines are the forces it feels: separation, alignment, cohesion. Click another boid to follow it instead. Each boid reacts only to the neighbors inside that circle, and flocks form anyway.

In SPH, each particle follows the same pattern: look at nearby particles, compute local forces, update. Instead of steering rules, SPH uses forces derived from fluid physics: how crowded is it here? Which way is the pressure pushing me? How fast are my neighbors moving compared to me? Fluid-like behavior emerges from these local interactions.

The Smoothing Kernel

The "smoothed" in SPH comes from the kernel function, which says how much a particle should care about a neighbor at a given distance.

A particle at the center of its smoothing radius cares most about particles right next to it, and cares less about particles further away. Beyond the smoothing radius, influence drops to zero.

Kernel function
r = distance(i, j)     // distance between particles
q = r / h               // normalized distance
W = (q < 1) ? (1 - q)³ : 0  // cubic falloff, zero beyond h
Figure 1. Each neighbor is labeled with its kernel weight, and the same neighbors sit on the curve at right. The weight falls off fast: a neighbor halfway to the dashed radius already counts for only an eighth. Drag a neighbor (or focus it and use the arrow keys) and the equation fills in with its distance.

The specific shape of the kernel matters less than its properties: it's smooth, it falls off to zero at the smoothing radius, and in a full simulation it is scaled by a constant so the total influence integrates to 1. The figure leaves that constant out so the weights read as 0 to 1.

Estimating Density

First each particle needs to know how crowded it is where it sits. This is the density at the particle's location. A particle estimates its density by summing up kernel-weighted contributions from all neighbors within the smoothing radius. Every particle in these figures has mass 1, and a particle counts itself, at distance zero and so with weight 1.

Density estimation
density[i] = 0
for each neighbor j within h:
  density[i] += mass[j] * W(r, h)
Figure 2. Particles on the crowded left come out darker than the ones spread out on the right. The ringed particle's sum is written out in the equation, starting with its own 1. Click or tap another particle to select it, or drag any particle to change the crowding.

Pressure: Pushing Apart

Fluids resist compression. When particles are packed too tightly, they push apart. A particle's pressure is a stiffness k times how far its density sits above a rest density ρ0, and the pressure force on a particle points away from regions of high density.

Pressure force (probe particle)
P[i] = k * (ρ[i] - ρ₀)  // equation of state
force[i] -= m[j] * (P[i]+P[j]) / (2*ρ[j]) * ∇W
Figure 3. The probe particle circles inside an uneven ring of neighbors. Red arrows show pressure forces, and the probe's arrow swings away from whichever side is more crowded. The dashed circle is the smoothing radius. Drag any neighbor, or uncheck "Animate probe" to drag the probe too.

Raise the rest density past a particle's density and its pressure goes negative, so it pulls on its neighbors instead of pushing. The full simulation at the end of this article clamps pressure at zero to avoid that clumping.

Viscosity: Smoothing Velocities

Pressure alone gives you something like an ideal gas. Real fluids have viscosity, which makes neighboring particles tend to move at similar speeds. The viscosity force is proportional to the velocity difference between a particle and its neighbors. For ∇²W we use the kernel's second derivative, 6(1 − r/h)/h², which is never negative, so the force always drags a particle's velocity toward its neighbors'.

Viscosity force
force[i] += μ * m[j]/ρ[j] * (v[j] - v[i]) * ∇²W(r, h)

Updating Positions

We now have all the forces. At each time step, we update velocity and position. The order of operations and the number of intermediate evaluations determine how accurately the integration tracks the true path. Here are four methods, from simplest to most accurate, each sent around the same circular orbit.

Symplectic Euler update, and each method's energy error
// Explicit Euler: position first, using the old velocity
pos += vel * dt;  vel += F/ρ * dt
// Symplectic Euler: velocity first, then position (shown above)
vel += F/ρ * dt;  pos += vel * dt
// Verlet: next position from the last two positions
// RK4: four force evaluations per step
Figure 5. Four integration methods on the same orbit (dashed), with each one's energy error in the row above. Explicit Euler gains energy on every step and spirals outward. Symplectic Euler does the same work in the other order and stays on a closed loop, which is why the simulations in this series use it.
