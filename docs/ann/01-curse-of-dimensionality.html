The Curse of Dimensionality

Why exact nearest-neighbor search stops paying off in high dimensions, and why practical systems settle for approximate answers.

Part 1 of Approximate Nearest Neighbors

Nearest neighbors in two dimensions

Nearest-neighbor search starts from a set of points and a query, and returns the points closest to the query. "Closest" usually means Euclidean distance, and in two dimensions we can check the answer by eye.

In the figure below the query point follows the cursor and its k nearest points are highlighted. The dashed circle passes through the k-th nearest point, so everything inside it is a neighbor.

Move the cursor over the plot, tap it, or focus it and use the arrow keys.

Figure 1. The k closest points to the query, found by measuring the distance to all 120 points and keeping the k smallest. Notice that the circle grows where the points are sparse and shrinks where they are dense: the neighborhood has a fixed count, not a fixed size.

The figure computes its answer the direct way: measure the distance from the query to every point, sort, and return the k smallest. This is exact search. It is the only way to be sure we have the true nearest neighbors without any other structure, and it costs one distance computation per point, so the work grows linearly with the size of the set.

Skipping most of the points with a kd-tree

We can do better than checking every point. A kd-tree recursively splits the space along alternating axes, at the median point each time, until each leaf region holds a handful of points. To answer a query we descend to the leaf containing it, scan that leaf, and then check another branch only if it could contain a point closer than the best one found so far.

Move the cursor over the plot, tap it, or focus it and use the arrow keys.

Figure 2. A kd-tree over 80 points. The teal leaves are the ones the search opened for this query; the unshaded leaves were skipped because the splitting line between them and the query is farther away than the nearest point already found. Move the query next to a split line and a second leaf has to be opened. Deepen the tree and the visited share drops, because each leaf holds fewer points.

The savings come from that skipping step, called pruning. Once the search holds a candidate at distance r, any branch whose region is farther than r from the query cannot improve the answer. The result is still exact. With N points in two dimensions a query visits a few leaves out of N / (leaf size), so the cost grows roughly with log N instead of N. For low-dimensional data such as map coordinates, kd-trees are still a standard tool.

What changes in high dimensions?

The vectors we want to search in machine learning are much longer than two numbers. A sentence embedding typically has 384, 768 or 1536 dimensions, and an image embedding can have 2048. Does the kd-tree's pruning carry over?

Mostly it does not, because of two related effects in the geometry of high-dimensional space. As the dimension grows, volume moves away from the center of a region toward its corners, and the distances between random points become nearly equal. We look at each in turn, and then run a kd-tree in higher dimensions to measure what they do to it.

Volume moves to the corners

Take a cube of side 2 in d dimensions and inscribe a ball of radius 1 in it. In two dimensions the ball (a disc) fills π/4, about 79%, of the square. In three dimensions it fills about 52% of the cube. In ten dimensions it fills a quarter of one percent. The ball still touches every face of the cube, yet nearly all of the volume lies outside it, in the corners.

Figure 3. Left: the square stands for the cube's whole volume and the pink disc's area is the share of it inside the inscribed ball. At d = 2 this is the literal picture; above that it is an area chart, since we cannot draw the shape. Right: the same share on a log scale, computed from Vball / Vcube = πd/2 / (Γ(d/2 + 1) · 2d). Each added dimension costs more than the one before; by d = 20 the ball holds about one part in forty million.

If we sample points uniformly inside a high-dimensional cube, almost none of them land near the center, because almost none of the volume is there. This is why high-dimensional data is often described as living on a thin shell.

Distances concentrate

The effect that matters most for search is on distances. Generate random points in d dimensions and collect the distance between every pair. In two dimensions those distances vary a lot: some pairs nearly touch and some sit in opposite corners. As d grows, each distance becomes a sum over many independent coordinates, the sum averages out, and the distances bunch up around their mean.

Figure 4. All 44,850 pairwise distances between 300 points drawn uniformly from the unit cube, divided by their mean so that different dimensions share an axis. Gray is d = 2; pink is the dimension on the slider. Each histogram is scaled to its own peak. The dashed lines mark the closest and farthest pair at the current d: slide d up and they close in on the mean from both sides.

A nearest-neighbor search depends on the nearest point being much closer than a typical point. When the closest and farthest pairs differ by a small factor, a bound like "this region is at least r away" stops ruling much out.

Uniform random points are the hard case. Real embeddings cluster by topic or content, so a query's true neighbors are usually much closer than a random point is, and that local structure is what the methods in the rest of this series exploit. The ambient geometry is still the one shown here.

Why kd-tree pruning stops working

The kd-tree skipped a branch when the query was farther from that branch's region than from its current best candidate. Two things undo this in high dimensions. The best candidate is not much closer than everything else, which is the distance concentration above. And the tree runs out of splits: 2,000 points with 8 to a leaf make a tree about 8 levels deep, so in 16 dimensions half of the axes are never split at all. Every region spans the full width of the cube along those axes, and the few axes that were split rarely put a region farther away than the candidate.

Figure 5 measures this. It builds a kd-tree over 2,000 uniform random points in each dimension, runs 30 exact queries, and records how many points the search had to examine.

Figure 5. Share of the dataset an exact kd-tree search examines, measured in this page for 2,000 uniform random points (leaves of 8, mean over 30 random queries). At low d the tree skips nearly everything. Somewhere between 10 and 20 dimensions it ends up reading almost every point, which is a linear scan with the cost of walking the tree added on top.

The crossover depends on the data: more points push it to the right, and clustered data prunes better than uniform data. It does not move far enough to reach the hundreds of dimensions an embedding has.

Giving up exactness

That leaves two options. We can accept linear scans, whose cost grows in step with the collection: a million 768-dimensional vectors means 768 million multiply-adds per query. Or we can stop insisting on the exact answer and return approximate nearest neighbors, which means some true neighbors will occasionally be missed. Most vector search systems take the second path, and measure what they lose as recall: the fraction of the true k nearest neighbors that the search returned.

The rest of this series covers three ideas that such systems combine. Partitioning cuts the space into regions and searches only the ones near the query. Compression replaces full vectors with small codes that are cheap to compare. Hardware-aware design lays the index out for the device it lives on, whether RAM, SSD or GPU.

The next article starts with partitioning, which is the kd-tree's idea again with one change: the regions follow the data instead of the axes, and the search is allowed to be right most of the time instead of every time.
