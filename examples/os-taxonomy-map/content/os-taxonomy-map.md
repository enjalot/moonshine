Marble [published an interesting open dataset](https://github.com/withmarbleapp/os-taxonomy) of what
children learn across the primary years: 1,590 micro-topics, each a single teachable idea like
*Unit fractions* or *What Is a Volcano*, wired together by 3,221 prerequisite edges, each edge
carrying a one-line reason.

This article explores the dataset by making a semantic map of the topics. Each topic's name and description was embedded with a sentence-embedding model, then projected from 768 dimensions down to two with UMAP, using
[Latent Scope](https://github.com/enjalot/latent-scope). Topics whose descriptions are similar land
near each other. The layout was never shown the subject labels, the age ranges, or the prerequisite
graph. Whatever structure appears below, the model found it in the words alone.

## Continents of the curriculum

::::scrolly-tour{id=taxonomy-tour}
:::step
Each of these 1,590 dots is one micro-topic. Right now the map is just a shape: a broad landmass
with bays and peninsulas. The structure is already here. We just can't read it yet.
:::

:::step
We color each dot by its subject and the shape resolves into clear clusters. Science fills the north.
Mathematics holds the west. English runs along the south, and History sits on the east coast.

The placement of the dots had no awareness of the subjects. Topics about plants just read more like each other than
topics about commas or historical figures. The smaller subjects such as Personal & Social Development, Life Skills,
Computing, and Learning to Learn gather in the interior between Mathematics and English.
:::

:::step
Zoom into the east coast and the continent resolves into towns. Ancient Egypt forms its own cluster
of 31 topics, next door to Ancient Greece and Rome.

Notice the green dot among the brown. *Pompeii & Vesuvius* is filed under Science in the taxonomy,
but the model shelved it here, with the Romans. Its volcano prerequisites live on the far side of
the map.
:::

:::step
We can see those dependencies in the corner of the science continent.
:::

:::step
Some towns sit right on a border. *Coins & Notes* is a Life Skills topic. *Coin Values* is
Mathematics. They are nearest neighbors on the map because they are about the same thing; only a
department boundary separates them in the files.

The map can reveal subtle  connections where the subject labels draw borders.
:::

:::step
Now we color by starting age instead. Topics in dark blue for age 4 or 5: counting, pencil grip, naming
feelings. Older topics like Single-cell organisms or simplifying fractions are brighter.

## Exploring the full taxonomy map

The taxonomy's 3,221 prerequisite edges were authored topic by topic, each with a written reason. We didn't show those to the embedding model, yet drawn on the map, 95% of edges connect two topics of the
same subject, and the median edge spans about 2% of the map's width. Things that must be learned
together are usually about the same thing, so the handmade graph and the semantic layout agree almost
everywhere.

That agreement makes the exceptions easy to spot. The longest edges on the map nearly all leave
Ancient Egypt. :term[Egyptian Maths and Engineering]{to=explorer.egyptian-maths} reaches across to
:term[Unit fractions]{to=explorer.unit-fractions};
:term[Pyramids and the Great Sphinx]{to=explorer.pyramids} reaches to
:term[3-D shapes]{to=explorer.three-d-shapes}. To study how pyramids were surveyed, a learner needs
mathematics from the other side of the map. The explorer below opens with that topic selected so you
can see its supply lines.



:::figure{id=explorer}

generated by a language model and are signposts, not ground truth.
:::

## Making your own map

If you enjoyed exploring an interesting dataset in this way, you can make your own with [Latent Scope](https://github.com/enjalot/latent-scope) by pointing your agent at it and asking to map your data. Then you can make a custom interactive article like this with the  [Moonshine](https://github.com/enjalot/moonshine) skill.
